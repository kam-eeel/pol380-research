# Eurobarometer volume choice

Eurobarometer data sets come in several volumes (A/AA/AP/AAP/B/BP/C/D). Each volume provides a different degree of resolution or type of comparison for the results. For this analysis, volume A is the most relevant because it directly provides country-level results. All other volumes look at either groups of countries, trends over time, and results broken down by socio-demographics. Because country and wave fixed effects absorb time-invariant national traits and demographic composition, the analysis needs only the outcome variable at the country level.

# Exposure — Criterion D (independence)

Criterion D (criterion A corroborated by two or more independent sources) is computed programmatically rather than hand-logged. The register records A, B, and C per source. D is derived for each country-window as the presence of at least two distinct authors among the sources that establish A there.
At data entry, every date and country attribution in the register was taken only from the portion of a source that reports its own primary research. No attribution was taken from background sections or from passages that discuss other investigations. A logged attribution is therefore never a cited, reprinted, or reused one. As a result, the sources that establish A for a given cell are independent evidentiary bases by construction. Accordingly, the cluster of late-September 2022 disclosures surrounding Meta's takedown announcement (EU DisinfoLab, DFRLab, Meta, and the Institute for Strategic Dialogue) counts as four independent sources, not one. Each conducted its own investigation, and only its own findings were logged. The register contains a single sponsor overlap: the Federal Foreign Office and the Bavarian Office for the Protection of the Constitution, which are both German state bodies. This overlap affects no coded cell, because the former is Germany-only and other authors corroborate each of the latter's attributed countries.

# Exposure — Tier assignment gap (A with partial evidence, no corroboration)

The codebook's tier table does not explicitly cover three combinations in which criterion A is met but the remaining criteria are only partly present. These combinations are A with B alone, A with C alone, and A with D alone. They satisfy neither tier-2 rule nor the tier-1 rule as literally written ("A met, none of B, C, or D"). The tier-2 rules require either corroboration paired with at least one content criterion or both content criteria together. Such cells are assigned tier 1.
The rationale derives from the general logic of the table. A gates any positive score. Tier 2 ("secondary target") is reserved for a country that shows either corroborated content activity or the full B-and-C content pair. Tier 3 is reserved for the complete A-B-C-D profile. A single uncorroborated signal does not clear the tier-2 bar and remains marginal or incidental. Such a signal is either one content criterion without corroboration or bare corroboration of naming with no documented content.

# Exposure — Analysis window truncation

The panel is truncated to windows 1 through 7 (Eurobarometer 97.5 through 103.3), even though the collected Eurobarometer data extend through window 9 (105.2). The research could not find forensic activity for any country during windows 8 and 9. The time period for the research was constrained further than initially expected. Under decision rule R10, a 0 in the exposure index means "no documented activity in the frozen register for this window," not "no activity occurred." Coding windows 8 and 9 as zeros would therefore not measure a decline in exposure. Instead, it would inject a block of artefactual zeros that reflect reporting lag. These zeros would misrepresent the campaign as having ceased and distort any within-country trajectory that ends in those windows.

# DV — Normalization

The two outcome variables, support_aid and support_sanctions, are operationalized as total_agree ("Tout à fait d'accord" plus "Plutôt d'accord") as reported in Volume A. "Do not know" stays in the denominator. The renormalized alternative, support as a share of valid opinion-holders (agree over agree-plus-disagree), is kept only as a robustness specification.

The choice follows from the mechanism under study. Doppelgänger-style influence operations do not act solely by converting agreement into disagreement. A substantial part of their documented effect is to manufacture doubt and demobilize settled opinion, which moves respondents from a committed position into "don't know." The research question asks whether campaign activity erodes public support. For that question, erosion achieved by demobilization is erosion, and the as-reported measure registers it as such: an agreer who becomes a don't-know lowers total_agree. Renormalization partially removes this channel because a respondent who leaves agreement for don't-know exits both the numerator and the denominator. As a result, the ratio moves less than underlying support actually did.

The standard motivation for renormalizing is to net out cross-national differences in don't-know propensity, whether from political culture, survey translation, or item salience. This motivation does not apply strongly here, because the two-way fixed effects design already absorbs those differences. Country fixed effects remove any time-invariant national tendency toward non-response, and wave fixed effects remove common shocks to it. The estimator therefore already handles the confound that renormalization is meant to address, which inverts the trade-off. In this design, renormalization mainly sacrifices within-country signal rather than removing bias.

The as-reported measure is also the more interpretable and defensible quantity. It is the
standard Eurobarometer topline, so a coefficient reads directly as the change in
percentage-point support associated with a one-tier change in exposure. This reading matches
how these figures are publicly reported. The renormalized version estimates support
conditional on holding an opinion, which is both more awkward to state and less standard.

The limitation is that the as-reported measure conflates persuasion (agree to disagree) with demobilization (agree to don't-know). Because the research question does not require separating these channels, this limitation is acceptable. Two checks guard against the concern. First, the renormalized DV is run as a robustness column to confirm that the result is not an artefact of the denominator choice. Second, the don't-know share may be modelled as a secondary outcome. A finding that exposure simultaneously raises the don't-know share and lowers support would evidence the demobilization channel directly rather than leaving it asserted.

# Control — GDP per capita (real)

Real GDP per capita controls for prosperity. Prosperity plausibly shapes both a country's attractiveness as a disinformation target and its public's willingness to bear the cost of aid and sanctions. Source: Eurostat nama_10_pc, item B1GQ, unit CLV20_EUR_HAB (chain-linked volumes, constant prices), matched by wave year. The measure uses constant prices to avoid double-counting inflation.

# Control — Inflation (HICP)

Inflation is a live confounder in this window: cost-of-living pressure may turn publics against sanctions as self-harming, independently of disinformation exposure. Source: Eurostat prc_hicp_manr (annual rate of change).

# Control — Ukrainian displaced-population share

The displaced-population share is the most direct confounder. Hosting a large Ukrainian population raises war salience and solidarity while plausibly shifting exposure and receptivity to Russian narratives. Without this control, exposure could proxy involvement in the war. The share is constructed as temporary-protection beneficiaries (Eurostat migr_asytpsm, citizen UA) over population (demo_pjan) at the fieldwork-midpoint month.
Two caveats apply. The share counts registered grants, not all Ukrainians present. It is also structurally zero before March 2022, so the W1 values (fieldwork June-July 2022) reflect only about four months of arrivals. Germany reports from August 2022 only, so its W1 value uses the August 2022 count, one month after the W1 fieldwork midpoint. Until October 30, 2026, that cell was filled with zero; the cached controls were patched for that cell alone, so later Eurostat revisions to other cells do not enter.

# Control — Population denominator (shared)

Eurostat demo_pjan (January 1, sex T, age TOTAL) is the denominator for both the displaced share and the public service media (PSM) per-capita moderator (2021). Using one population source keeps the two figures internally consistent.

# Controls — Set composition and two deliberate omissions

The set (real GDP per capita, inflation, displaced share) is the conventional prosperity, inflation, and war-exposure trio, and each has a confounding path to both exposure and the outcome.
Two omissions are deliberate. First, the coalition Russia-friendliness control was dropped as effectively time-invariant (absorbed by country fixed effects) and disproportionately costly. However, a Moscow-sympathetic government is a substantive omission to defend. Second, no direct media-trust control is included; the PSM moderator addresses that dimension instead. All controls are justified only by within-country variation, because fixed effects absorb time-invariant traits.

# Moderator — PSM funding (source and construction)

The moderator is public service media funding strength, operationalized as per-capita public revenues of public service media in euros. Values are for 2021 (Malta: 2019) and cover all EU-27. The figures are transcribed from Figure 4 of the European Parliament study "Public financing of news media in the EU." The study reports total public revenues of public service broadcasters by member state, drawn from the European Audiovisual Observatory database. Per-capita values are obtained by dividing each total by 2021 population (Eurostat, demo_pjan, January 1, 2021). The construction reproduces the report's own published per-capita figures for Germany (103.0) and Romania (7.6), and its top-five (Germany, Denmark, Sweden, Finland, Austria) and bottom-five (Poland, Bulgaria, Luxembourg, Malta, Romania). The population-weighted EU mean comes out at 49.8, against the report's 49.7. This match serves as the validation check on both the transcribed totals and the population denominators, and the notebook asserts the Germany and Romania anchors.

The measure is a single-year (2021) snapshot. This choice is not an assumption that funding levels are constant over 2022-2025. It follows from the moderator's specification as a time-invariant country characteristic. Two-way fixed effects absorb any time-invariant national traits, so PSM cannot enter as a main effect and appears only through its interaction with exposure. The interaction does not require that funding levels stay constant. It requires only that each country's relative standing in the funding distribution hold across the window. This requirement is weaker: level constancy would imply stable rankings, but not the reverse. It is also one that the evidence supports, because institutional arrangements rather than annual budgets set cross-national funding rankings.

# Inference — Equivalence Bounds (pre-committed)

The null result is tested for equivalence, not only for difference: a non-significant coefficient shows only that an effect was not detected, while an equivalence test asks whether effects of a stated, substantively meaningful size can be ruled out. The bounds below were fixed after the point estimates and analytic standard errors had been reported but before any equivalence test, bootstrap confidence interval, or bootstrap minimum detectable effect was computed. They are derived only from the outcome data and survey sample sizes, never from the exposure coefficient.

Bounds are stated on the change in support when a state moves from no documented exposure to the top tier of the index (0 to 3), the substantively meaningful contrast; Spain makes exactly this switch within the window. The bound on the per-index-point coefficient is that change divided by three, which relies on the linear coding of the index; the tier-dummy specification checks that assumption.

The primary bound is the full EU-wide decline in support over the window: the unweighted mean across the 27 states fell from 67.98 to 59.56 percent for military aid and from 74.84 to 70.31 percent for sanctions between W1 and W7, so Δ = 8.42 points for aid and 4.54 for sanctions (differences taken before rounding; 2.81 and 1.51 per index point). Unweighted means are used because the two-way fixed effects estimator weights each state equally, and the window endpoints rather than the lowest wave are used to avoid selecting the largest drop. An effect of this size would mean that top-tier exposure alone moves a state's support by as much as everything that happened across the Union in two years; ruling it out answers whether documented Doppelgänger activity could account for the decline.

Four stricter bounds are reported as sensitivity checks, with a verdict at each. Half the EU-wide decline (4.21 aid, 2.27 sanctions) is closer to a smallest effect of interest, with the fraction a judgement call. The median 95 percent margin of error of a single country's topline (2.95 aid, 2.81 sanctions), computed from the actual Volume A sample sizes (500 to 1,559 respondents, median 1,011) under simple random sampling, marks an effect too small to register in a national poll. Hartman and Hidalgo's default of 0.36 standard deviations of the residualised untreated outcome (1.62 aid, 1.38 sanctions; σ is the residual standard deviation of the 140 unexposed country-waves after two-way fixed effects, 4.50 and 3.83) has published precedent but was designed for balance and placebo tests with a binary treatment. A Piccardi-sized effect (2.00 for both outcomes) takes the two-degree effect their field experiment was powered to detect on a 0–100 feeling thermometer; it is on a different scale and measured at the individual level under an intensive manipulation, so it serves as context, not as a substantive threshold.

The headline test is two one-sided tests (TOST) at α = 0.05, declaring equivalence when both H0: 3β ≤ −Δ and H0: 3β ≥ +Δ are rejected, equivalently when the 90 percent equal-tailed confidence interval for 3β lies inside ±Δ. A backlash, with exposure raising support, is substantively possible, so the two-sided version carries the claim; the one-sided result against erosion alone is reported alongside. Inference follows the bootstrap diagnostic: each one-sided test is a restricted wild cluster bootstrap (WCR) with the null β = ±Δ/3 imposed, 9,999 Rademacher draws, clustered by country, and the 90 and 95 percent intervals are obtained by inverting the same test over a grid of β. The equivalence confidence interval, the smallest symmetric bound at which equivalence would be declared, is reported so that readers can apply their own bound. Primary specification is the controlled model (m2), with the uncontrolled model (m1) alongside, on the full W1–W7 panel. The minimum detectable effect at 80 percent power is computed from the bootstrap interval as (t.975,26 + t.80,26) × SE*, where SE* is the bootstrap 95 percent interval's half-width divided by t.975,26.

# Inference — Timing and Placebo Specifications (pre-committed)

The lag regresses opinion at wave t on exposure at t−1 and is estimated on W2–W7. W1 is dropped rather than assigned a lagged value, because no observation window precedes it. Six states (EE, ES, HU, LT, LV, SK) identify it. France, Italy, and Poland drop out because their only change in exposure falls in W7. As a labelled sensitivity check, the lag is also estimated on W1–W7 with pre-invasion exposure set to zero; the campaign did not exist before 24 February 2022, so that value is true. This version is not primary, because most of its added identification comes from that assumed starting value. It brings in Germany, France, Italy, and Poland through a jump between W1 and W2, for ten identifying states in total.

_Corrected on October 30, 2026: the original entry also listed Latvia among the added states, but Latvia already identifies the W2–W7 lag._

The joint model enters contemporaneous and lagged exposure together on W2–W7. It reports both coefficients and their sum, the cumulative effect, which is estimated directly as the coefficient on exposure after reparameterising the lag as the difference between lagged and current exposure.

The placebo regresses opinion at wave t on exposure at t+1, conditional on contemporaneous exposure, on W1–W6. Because exposure is persistent, the lead entered alone would also absorb any contemporaneous effect. The conditional form asks the placebo question directly: does future documented activity predict current opinion? The lead alone is reported alongside. France, Italy, and Poland identify the lead only through the W7 coding: their lead switches at W6 only because W7 is coded 1, and if that drop reflects reporting lag their lead values are artefacts. The conditional lead is therefore also reported without those three states.

Inference follows the bootstrap diagnostic. Every specification here is identified by six states or relies on single-wave changes, so WR is reported alongside WCR throughout, with B = 9,999, seed 0, and intervals obtained by test inversion. Verdicts use WCR, which the diagnostic shows is conservative in six-state designs. Where WR would give a different verdict, the text says so.

The lag and the cumulative effect are tested for equivalence exactly as the contemporaneous effect is. The bounds are the pre-committed ones above, on the 0 to 3 change: the full EU-wide decline as primary, and half the decline, the poll margin of error, 0.36σ, and a Piccardi-sized effect as sensitivity bounds. The headline is TOST, with the one-sided test against erosion alongside. The bounds concern the size of an effect, not its timing, so they apply unchanged to a delayed or accumulated effect.

The placebo is framed as an equivalence test (Hartman and Hidalgo 2018), so a non-significant lead is not treated as evidence of no pre-trend (Roth 2022). The verdict is TOST at the primary bound (8.42 for aid, 4.54 for sanctions). Hartman and Hidalgo's 0.36σ default (1.62, 1.38) is reported as a sensitivity bound, although at the interval widths seen in the main specification it cannot pass whatever the data show. The equivalence confidence interval is reported as the smallest pre-trend, in either direction, that the data rule out. If that magnitude is too large to be informative, the placebo is described as uninformative rather than as passed.

# Inference — Functional Form, Jackknife, and Heterogeneity-Robust Checks (pre-committed)

The index realises only the values 0, 1, and 3, so the linear coding is tested by replacing it with separate dummies for tier 1 and tier 3. The tier-1 dummy varies in eight states. The tier-3 dummy varies in five (Spain, Latvia, France, Italy, and Poland), the last three only through their W7 change. Linearity is tested as γ = β₃ − 3β₁, estimated as the coefficient on the tier-3 dummy when it is added to the linear model. β₃ is the 0-to-3 contrast without any linearity assumption. It is tested for equivalence at the pre-committed bounds directly, without dividing by three: TOST as the headline and the one-sided erosion test alongside. The tier-3 dummy is identified by five states, so WR is reported alongside WCR and verdicts use WCR.

The leave-one-country-out jackknife drops each of the nine states whose exposure varies, and two groups: the Baltic states (Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania) together, and France, Italy, and Poland together, whose only change falls in W7. For each sample it reports the coefficient, the WCR and WR p-values, the 90 percent interval, the equivalence confidence interval, and TOST and the erosion test at the primary bound. This is done for both outcomes, with and without controls.

The robustness table's remaining analytic p-values are replaced with bootstrap ones. R4 (country-specific linear trends) uses WCR with the trends entered as controls. R8 (leave-one-country-out over all 27 states) uses WCR for every dropped country. A WR p-value is added to every row.

Following de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille (2020), the two-way fixed effects weights are reported for the ordered treatment: the exposure residual from the fixed effects, and the controls where present, times exposure, normalised over the treated cells. Both robustness measures are reported. The first is the smallest dispersion of cell-level effects compatible with the true average effect being zero. The second, which exists because some weights are negative, is the smallest dispersion compatible with every cell-level effect having the opposite sign to the estimate.

Their heterogeneity-robust estimator DID_M is also estimated, generalised to the ordered index per unit of exposure. Each state whose tier changes between consecutive waves is compared with the states that stayed at its starting tier over the same two waves. Its change in support minus the stayers' mean change is signed by the direction of the switch, summed, and divided by the total absolute change in tier. With a binary treatment this is exactly their DID_M. Switches with no stayer at the starting tier are left out. Joiners (increases) and leavers (decreases) are also reported separately. DID_M is estimated without controls. Inference is a percentile 90 percent interval from a bootstrap that resamples countries (999 draws), together with a permutation p-value that reassigns the real exposure paths to random countries, as in the bootstrap diagnostic (4,002 draws). Few states switch, so DID_M is reported as an indicative check on whether effect heterogeneity could hide erosion, without an equivalence verdict.

# Data — Individual-level Eurobarometer files (not pursued)

The respondent-level Eurobarometer files held by GESIS were considered as a supplementary data source and not pursued. Exposure is coded per country and observation window, so it does not vary among respondents interviewed in the same country-wave. Respondent-level data would therefore add no identifying variation for the effect that the paper estimates. The coefficient and its uncertainty would remain those of the country-wave panel, still clustered by country and still identified by the same nine states.

What the files would add is a different question: whether effects differ across kinds of respondents. For example, effects could differ between frequent and infrequent users of social media, the channel through which Doppelgänger content was distributed. That is a question about who is affected rather than whether documented activity moved public opinion. Answering it would change the paper's research question rather than strengthen its answer to the current one.

Two points are addressed substantively instead. First, the ecological-inference objection does not apply with force here. The treatment is assigned at the country-wave level, and the outcome is a population share. The estimand is therefore the population-average effect, which the country toplines identify directly. Second, dilution is acknowledged and quantified. If only a fraction of a state's population encountered the content, the population-level bound on erosion implies a correspondingly larger bound among those reached. The limitations state that bound explicitly. Testing effects among the reached subgroup is identified as the natural follow-up study.
